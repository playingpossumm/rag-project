§ 01The problem this solves

Why you cannot simply paste your documents into a chatbot.

A language model knows what was in its training data. It does not know what is in your files, and it cannot be told everything — there is a hard limit on how much text you can hand it at once, called the context window. Twenty research papers is already far past that limit for most practical purposes, and a real document set is far bigger.

So you cannot give the model everything. You have to give it the right few pages. Finding those pages, before the model ever runs, is the entire job of this system. The pattern has a name:

In plain terms

Search your documents first. Take only the handful of passages that look relevant. Hand those to the model and tell it to answer from them and nothing else — and to say so if the answer is not there.

The term

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). "Retrieval" is the search half; "augmented generation" is the model writing an answer from what was retrieved rather than from memory.

"It's a RAG pipeline — retrieve first, then generate strictly from the retrieved context."
Why it matters

It is the difference between a model that recalls and one that cites. Because the answer is assembled from passages you can point at, every claim has a source, and a wrong answer is diagnosable: either retrieval failed to find the evidence, or generation mishandled evidence it was given. Those are different bugs with different fixes.

The design choice that defines this build

Generation is optional here, and off by default. Ask a question and you get the source passages back verbatim, with citations — no model writes anything. That costs nothing, requires no API key, and cannot hallucinate, because nothing is being composed.

For contracts, policies and specifications this is often the better answer anyway: the exact wording is the thing that matters, and a paraphrase is a downgrade. Writing prose is a layer on top that you switch on.

§ 02How a document becomes searchable

Everything expensive happens once, offline. Asking a question never touches your PDFs.

Splitting: why documents get cut up

In plain terms

A whole document is too big to be a useful search result — you would get back "it's in this 40-page paper somewhere". So each document is cut into overlapping passages of a few hundred words, and those become the things you search.

They overlap deliberately. A definition sitting exactly on a cut would be sliced in half; the overlap means it survives whole in the neighbouring passage.

The term

Chunking, with a stride and an overlap. Here: 210 tokens per chunk, 40 overlap. A token is roughly ¾ of a word — it is how models actually count text.

"Chunked at 210 tokens with 40 overlap, page-scoped so citations stay unambiguous."
Why those numbers, and a warning

Not preference — a hard ceiling. The model that turns text into vectors accepts 256 tokens and silently discards everything past it. No error, no warning.

This system shipped that bug once. Chunks were sized at 500 words, which is about 626 tokens, so the median chunk lost 370 tokens before it was ever indexed. The text was still stored and still displayed — it simply could not influence whether its own passage was ever found. Roughly 60% of the corpus was unfindable but not unusable, which is exactly the kind of failure nobody notices.

Embedding: turning meaning into coordinates

In plain terms

Each passage is converted into a list of 384 numbers. Think of it as a position on a map, where the map is arranged by meaning: passages about similar things end up near each other, even when they share no words at all.

"Revenue increased" and "sales grew" land as close neighbours despite having nothing in common letter by letter.

The term

An embedding or dense vector, produced by a bi-encoder — so called because it encodes the question and the passage separately. Similarity is cosine similarity: the angle between two of these positions.

"Bi-encoder embeddings, 384 dimensions, cosine similarity over a FAISS flat index."
Why it has a ceiling

Because the passage is squashed into one point before anyone asks a question, everything it says is averaged together. A long precise passage gets diluted by its own surrounding prose, while a short passage repeating the topic word looks intensely relevant.

That is the structural weakness the next three stages exist to correct, and it is why the system does not stop here.

§ 03What happens when you ask

Six stages, each fixing a specific weakness in the one before it. The app shows you all six for every answer.

Two searches at once, not one

Embeddings are strong at meaning and weak at identity. A model number like P100, a dataset name like WMT 2014, an acronym — these carry meaning by being exactly themselves, and averaging them into 384 numbers washes them out. So a second, completely different search runs at the same time.

In plain terms

The second search counts word overlap, weighting rare words far above common ones. It cannot understand paraphrase at all — but it never loses a rare term. The two searches have opposite blind spots, so running both covers each one's gap.

The terms

Dense (meaning) and sparse (exact words) retrieval; the sparse one is BM25, weighted by IDF — inverse document frequency, i.e. rarity. Running both is hybrid search.

"Hybrid — dense plus BM25, fused with RRF."
Measured

Adding the second retriever moved hit rate from 0.788 to 0.864 — the largest single gain in the system.

Merging two disagreeing rankings

In plain terms

Now there are two ranked lists that disagree, and their scores cannot be compared — one is a similarity between −1 and 1, the other is an unbounded count. Adding them is meaningless.

So the scores are thrown away and only positions are used. Being ranked respectably by both retrievers beats being ranked first by one.

The term

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). Each retriever contributes 1 / (60 + rank) per passage, and the contributions are summed.

"RRF — scale-free, no weight to tune per corpus."
Why not weighted scores

The alternative normalises both score scales and blends them with a tunable knob. It measured about the same here — but it needs re-tuning whenever the corpus changes, and normalising per query destroys the confidence signal: a query where everything is mediocre still produces a top score of 1.0. RRF has nothing to get wrong.

Reading the question and the passage together

In plain terms

Everything so far compared a question to passages that were summarised long before the question existed. Now a second, slower model reads the question and each candidate passage side by side, and scores how well one answers the other.

It is far more accurate and far too slow to run over a whole library — so it only ever sees the ~20 candidates the first stage shortlisted.

The term

A cross-encoder reranker, the second half of two-stage retrieval. Cheap first stage for recall, expensive second stage for precision.

"Cross-encoder reranking over a 20-candidate shortlist."
Measured

MRR 0.601 → 0.710. MRR asks how high the first correct result sits, so this is the number that says "the right passage moved to the top", not merely "into the set". It costs about a second per question — by far the most expensive stage, and it earns it.

Not letting one document take every slot

In plain terms

The reranker scores each passage on its own, so when one document matches well it takes all five slots. If five papers answer your question, you hear from one.

So there is a cap: at most two passages from any single document.

The terms

A per-document diversity cap. The metric it protects is source recall — of the documents that could answer, how many actually came back.

"Capped at two per source, because we optimise source recall, not just hit rate."
An honest trade, not a free win

Source recall 0.742 → 0.773, and hit rate 0.864 → 0.848. It costs something: when only one document genuinely answers, capping it pushes a relevant passage out for an irrelevant one.

An earlier, smaller test set said the cap was free. It was not — the conclusion reversed when the test set grew.

Growing the passage back out

Ranking works best on small passages; reading works best on larger ones. So the winners are expanded to include their neighbouring text before you see them — called parent-document retrieval. Measured: context recall 0.742 → 0.833 for 2.4× the text. Expanding to whole pages gets 0.848 but costs 4.8× — so the window is the default, and pages remain available for when a citation must point at something a reader can verify in one place.

§ 04Knowing when to say no

The feature most products do not have, and the one most worth having.

Ask a document search system something its documents do not cover, and the honest answer is "this isn't in here". The usual behaviour is to return the closest topical match with total confidence, which is worse than useless — it looks exactly like a real answer.

In plain terms

The reranker's score is a genuine measure of "does this passage answer this question". When the best passage scores below a set line, the system refuses instead of answering.

The term

Abstention, at a calibrated threshold — calibrated meaning the line was chosen from measurements, not picked by eye.

"Calibrated abstention threshold at zero, tuned on 18 adversarial cases."
How the line was chosen

The test set includes 18 questions the corpus deliberately cannot answer. Answerable questions score a median of +4.93, unanswerable ones −2.33. The distributions overlap, so no line is clean and the choice is a real trade.

A subtlety worth being able to explain

The evaluation tool recommends a threshold of +2, and the system ships 0.0. That looks like ignoring the evidence. It is not.

The tool ranks thresholds by subtracting two rates — the fraction of unanswerable questions caught, minus the fraction of answerable ones wrongly refused. But those fractions come from populations of very different size: 18 against 66. That arithmetic quietly values one unanswerable question at 3.7 answerable ones.

Counted in actual questions, moving to +2 catches 4 more and wrongly refuses 4 more. Break-even, before you decide which error is worse — and refusing a question your documents can answer is clearly worse. So 0.0 stays.

"The net-separation column is Youden's J — it balances rates, and our class sizes are unbalanced, so we chose on counts."

§ 06Four times the measurement was right and the plan was wrong

The most useful thing in the project, stated as evidence rather than advice.

What sounded obviously rightWhat measuring showedOutcome
Label each passage with its document's title so the system can tell papers apart No ranking gain, and source recall fell 0.051 — every passage in a document got the same label, making them more alikeBuilt, measured, reverted

Tables retrieve badly and need special handling Tables measured easier than prose — 0.952 against 0.800No code written
Re-indexing is slow because the index is rebuilt each time Rebuilding the index is 0.01 s — 0% of the runtimeTask redefined

Three of those four would have produced correct, well-engineered code that improved nothing. The fourth was actively harmful and had already been written up as a success. Each measurement cost minutes.

Including bugs in the measuring tool itself

The evaluation tool's own NDCG once printed 1.373 — impossible, since the metric cannot exceed 1.0. It was caught only because a human recognised that the output violated a bound the metric is known to have.

A metric with no known bound would have shipped wrong and stayed wrong. That check is now an automated test, along with 21 others.

§ 07What is still wrong with it

Stated plainly, because a description that only lists wins is marketing.

It gets about 1 in 6 questions wrong, and 7 of them are structural

Running all 84 questions under six different configurations shows that 18 fail under at least one setting — but only 7 fail under every one. Those seven are unreachable by any amount of ranking work. Four share a shape: the question names a topic and a constraint, retrieval matches the topic, and the constraint — the part that decides which document is right — is averaged away.

Ask for "normalisation across features rather than examples" and you get Batch Normalization, which is the thing that phrase explicitly rules out. The fix is query decomposition — using a language model to break the question apart before searching — and it needs API credit the project does not currently have.

The document loaders have never run on real files

The corpus is 20 PDFs. Code exists for Word, PowerPoint, Excel and scanned documents, and none of it has ever processed a real file. The spreadsheet path is the least trustworthy: rows are flattened into Column: value text on an assumption with no evidence behind it. This is the largest unknown in the system and the cheapest to close.

Writing prose has never actually run

The generation layer is written and reaches the network, but no call has ever completed — the account has no credit. The code is real; the evidence is not, and this project's own record says those are different things.

§ 08Why build this instead of using a product

The honest answer is not "better answers".

A hand-built system will not beat NotebookLM or Glean on answer quality. They have better models, more compute, and teams working on nothing else. Competing there means losing on their terms.

They share a structural weakness, and it follows from being polished consumer products rather than being an oversight. They are closed systems that always sound confident.

"It doesn't beat a closed product on answer quality and isn't trying to. It competes on things they structurally can't offer: every retrieval stage is inspectable with scores, every change is measurable against 84 labelled cases, and it refuses when it doesn't know instead of answering anyway."

§ 09Glossary

chunk
One independently searchable passage, plus the record of which document and page it came from.
embedding / dense vector
A list of numbers encoding a text's meaning as a position in space. Here, 384 of them.
bi-encoder
Encodes question and passage separately, so passages can be processed in advance. Fast; never lets the two texts interact.
cross-encoder
Encodes them together for a much better relevance score. Too slow for a whole corpus — hence its use as a second stage.
BM25
Classic word-overlap scoring, weighting rare terms above common ones.
RRF
Reciprocal rank fusion. Merges rankings using positions only, never raw scores.
reranking
Re-scoring a shortlist with a slower, more accurate model.
context window
The hard limit on how much text a model can be given at once.
abstention
Refusing to answer when confidence falls below a calibrated line.
golden set
Questions with hand-labelled correct answers, used to score any change.
hallucination
A fluent, confident, false statement. Retrieval-only output cannot produce one, because nothing is composed.
query decomposition
Splitting a question into parts before searching. The unfinished fix for the seven structural failures.
