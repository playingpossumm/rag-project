Chapter 01The problem

Why you cannot simply paste your documents into a chatbot.

A language model knows what was in its training data. It does not know what is in your files, and it cannot be handed everything — there is a hard limit on how much text it can read at once, called the context window. Twenty research papers already exceeds any practical budget, and a real document set is far larger.

So the model has to be given the right few pages. Finding those pages, before the model runs at all, is the whole job of this system.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Search the documents first. Take the handful of passages that look relevant. Hand only those to the model, and instruct it to answer from them and nothing else.

"It's a RAG pipeline — retrieve first, then generate strictly from the retrieved context."

The consequence is not just economy. It is the difference between a model that recalls and one that cites. Because the answer is assembled from passages you can point at, every claim has a source, and a wrong answer becomes diagnosable: either retrieval failed to surface the evidence, or generation mishandled evidence it was given. Different bugs, different fixes.

The choice that defines this build

Generation is optional here and off by default. Ask a question and you get the source passages back verbatim, with citations. No model writes anything. It costs nothing, needs no API key, and cannot hallucinate, because nothing is being composed.

For contracts, policies and specifications that is often the better answer anyway: the exact wording is the thing that matters, and a paraphrase is a downgrade. Writing prose is a layer switched on top.

Chapter 02Turning documents into something searchable

All the expensive work happens once, offline. Asking a question never touches your PDFs.

Splitting: why documents get cut up

A whole document is a useless search result — you would get back "it's somewhere in this 40-page paper". So each document is cut into overlapping passages of a few hundred words, and those become the searchable units.

They overlap deliberately. A definition sitting exactly on a cut would be sliced in half; the overlap means it survives whole inside the neighbouring passage.

Chunking, with a stride and an overlap

Here: 210 tokens per passage with 40 tokens of overlap, so each new passage starts 170 tokens after the last. A token is roughly three-quarters of a word — it is how models actually count text.

"Chunked at 210 tokens, 40 overlap, page-scoped so citations stay unambiguous."
The bug this system already shipped

Those numbers are not preference, they are a ceiling. The model that turns text into vectors accepts 256 tokens and silently discards everything past it. No error, no warning.

Passages were originally sized at 500 words — about 626 tokens — so the median passage lost 370 tokens before it was ever indexed. That text was still stored, still displayed, still returned once its passage was selected. It simply could not influence whether that passage was ever found. Roughly 60% of the corpus was unfindable but not unusable: the exact kind of failure that produces no symptom.

Fixed by measuring in tokens, and by an assertion that now refuses to build an index at all if any passage exceeds the ceiling.

Embedding: turning meaning into coordinates

Each passage is converted into a list of 384 numbers — a position in a space arranged by meaning, where passages about similar things land near each other even when they share no words at all. "Revenue increased" and "sales grew" are near neighbours despite zero overlapping vocabulary.

Embedding · dense vector · bi-encoder

The vector is the embedding. The model producing it is a bi-encoder — so called because it encodes the question and the passage separately. Closeness is cosine similarity: the angle between two positions.

"Bi-encoder embeddings, 384 dimensions, cosine similarity over a FAISS flat index."

This is also where the ceiling on quality comes from. Because the passage is compressed into one point before anyone asks a question, everything it says is averaged together. A long, precise passage gets diluted by its own surrounding prose, while a short passage that repeats the topic word looks intensely relevant. Every stage after this exists to correct that.

Chapter 03Searching two ways at once

Two methods with opposite blind spots, run in parallel.

Embeddings are strong at meaning and weak at identity. A model number like P100, a dataset name like WMT 2014, an acronym — these mean something by being exactly themselves, and averaging them into 384 numbers washes them out.

So a second, completely different search runs at the same moment. It counts word overlap, weighting rare words far above common ones. It cannot understand paraphrase at all, and it never loses a rare term.

Dense · sparse · BM25 · IDF · hybrid search

Dense retrieval is the meaning-based one. Sparse is the word-overlap one, and the specific algorithm is BM25, which weights each shared word by IDF — inverse document frequency, i.e. how rare it is. Running both is hybrid search.

"Hybrid — dense plus BM25, fused with RRF."

Chapter 04Merging two rankings that disagree

Their scores cannot be compared, so the scores get thrown away.

You now have two ranked lists that disagree. Merging them sounds like addition, but a cosine similarity of 0.63 and a BM25 score of 11.4 are not commensurable — one is bounded between −1 and 1, the other is unbounded and depends on corpus statistics. Adding them is meaningless.

The fix is to discard the scores entirely and use only positions.

Why not weighted scores

The alternative normalises both score scales and blends them with a tunable knob (alpha). It measured the same here — hit rate 0.864 either way.

RRF is the default anyway, on robustness rather than numbers. Weighted fusion needs re-tuning whenever the corpus changes, and its per-query normalisation destroys the confidence signal: a query where every candidate is mediocre still produces a top score of 1.0. RRF has nothing to get wrong.

Chapter 05Reading the question and the passage together

The most expensive stage in the system, and the one that earns it.

Everything so far compared your question against passages that were summarised into vectors long before the question existed. The passage was never allowed to know what was being asked of it.

Cross-encoder · reranking · two-stage retrieval

A cheap first stage for recall (is the answer in the pool at all?), then an expensive second stage for precision (is it at the top?).

"Cross-encoder reranking over a 20-candidate shortlist."

Measured: MRR 0.601 → 0.710. MRR asks how high the first correct result sits, so this is the number that says the right passage moved to the top rather than merely into the set. It costs about a second per question — by far the most expensive stage.

Chapter 06Stopping one document taking every slot

A metric that says 1.000 while the answer is a quarter complete.

The reranker scores each passage on its own merits, so when one document matches well it takes all five slots. If five papers answer your question, you hear from one.

The measurement that exposed it

For the question "which model achieved 28.4 BLEU", five papers report that figure. All five returned passages came from one of them.

MRR scored it 1.000. Source recall scored it 0.200. Ranking metrics call that a perfect result, because by their definition it is — which is the wrong definition when the goal is to compile every relevant source rather than find one.

Diversity cap · source recall

A per-document diversity cap takes at most N passages from any one document. Source recall is the metric it protects: of the documents that could answer, how many actually came back.

"Capped at two per source — we optimise source recall, not just hit rate."

Chapter 07Growing the passage back out

Ranking wants small passages. Reading wants large ones.

Small passages rank better: a short span averages into a vector that stays specific. But a small passage is often a poor thing to answer from — it can be cut mid-argument, missing the sentence that defines its own subject.

So ranking happens on small passages, and each survivor is then expanded to include its neighbouring text before you see it. The retriever gets specificity, the reader gets context.

Parent-document retrieval · context recall

Context recall is the metric here, and it is the one ranking cannot express: does the text actually returned contain the answer? A passage can come from the right page and still be cut before the sentence that matters.

Chapter 08Knowing when to refuse

The feature most products do not have, and the hardest one to tune.

Ask a document search system something its documents do not cover, and the honest answer is "this isn't in here". The usual behaviour is to return the closest topical match with complete confidence — which is worse than useless, because it looks exactly like a real answer.

The reranker's score is a genuine measure of "does this passage answer this question", so it can be compared against a line. Below the line, the system refuses.

The subtlety worth being able to explain

The evaluation tool recommends a threshold of +2. The system ships 0.0. That looks like ignoring the evidence, and it is not.

The tool ranks thresholds by subtracting two rates: the fraction of unanswerable questions caught, minus the fraction of answerable ones wrongly refused. Those fractions come from populations of very different size — 18 against 66 — so the arithmetic quietly values one unanswerable question at 3.7 answerable ones.

Counted in actual questions, moving to +2 catches 4 more and wrongly refuses 4 more. Break-even, before you even decide which error is worse — and refusing a question your documents can answer is clearly worse. So 0.0 stays.

"That column is Youden's J — it balances rates, and our class sizes are unbalanced, so we chose on counts."
One number, two different costs

The same threshold means different things depending on the path. Through the library API the flag is advisory — passages are returned either way, so a false refusal merely mislabels a good answer. Through the interface the answer is withheld and replaced with a refusal, so a false refusal loses an answer the documents contain.

The second is where the asymmetry bites, and it is the path a person uses.

Chapter 09How any of this is judged

The part that separates a system that works from one that merely runs.

Every claim in this document is a number, and every number comes from the same place: 84 questions written against these documents, each labelled with the pages that actually answer it. 66 answerable, 18 deliberately not. Change anything, re-run, and see whether the numbers move.

Golden set

Questions with hand-labelled correct answers. Relevance is judged at page level rather than passage level — coarser, but page labels survive a change to passage size, which is exactly when a comparable metric matters most.

Chapter 10Five times measurement beat the plan

The most valuable habit in the project, as evidence rather than advice.

Three of the first four would have produced correct, well-engineered code that improved nothing. One was actively harmful and had already been written up as a success. Each measurement cost minutes.

Two of the bugs were in the measuring tool itself

The evaluation tool's own NDCG once printed 1.373 — impossible, since the metric cannot exceed 1.0. It was caught only because a human recognised that the output violated a bound the metric is known to have. A metric with no known bound would have shipped wrong and stayed wrong.

Separately, the diversity cap was measured on a fusion setting the system does not actually serve, so the row every document quoted as "the default" described a configuration that had never run. Five numbers moved when it was corrected — none by more than 0.004, which is precisely why nobody noticed.

Both now have automated tests. The NDCG bound is one of 22 assertions that run in milliseconds.

Chapter 11What is still wrong with it

Stated plainly, because a description that only lists wins is marketing.

It gets about one question in six wrong, and seven of them are structural

Running all 84 questions under six different configurations shows that 18 fail under at least one setting — but only 7 fail under every one. Those seven are unreachable by any amount of ranking work.

Four share a shape: the question names a topic and a constraint, retrieval matches the topic, and the constraint — the part that decides which document is right — is averaged away. Ask for "normalisation across features rather than examples" and you get Batch Normalization, which is the thing that phrase explicitly rules out.

The fix is query decomposition: using a language model to break the question apart before searching. It needs API credit the project does not have. The seven cases are now a fixture that any attempted fix can be scored against in about 20 seconds.

The document loaders have never run on a real file

The corpus is 20 PDFs. Code exists for Word, PowerPoint, Excel and scanned documents, and none of it has ever processed a real file. The spreadsheet path is least trustworthy: rows are flattened into Column: value text on an assumption with no evidence behind it. This is the largest unknown in the system and the cheapest to close.

Writing prose has never actually run

The generation layer is written and reaches the network, but no call has ever completed — the account has no credit. The code is real; the evidence is not, and this project's own record insists those are different things.

Chapter 12Why build it at all

The honest answer is not "better answers".

A hand-built system will not beat NotebookLM or Glean on raw answer quality. They have better models, far more compute, and teams working on nothing else. Competing there means losing on their terms.

They share a structural weakness, and it follows from being polished consumer products rather than being an oversight: they are closed systems that always sound confident.

The sentence to lead with

"It doesn't beat a closed product on answer quality and isn't trying to. It competes on what they structurally can't offer: every retrieval stage is inspectable with scores, every change is measurable against 84 labelled cases, and it refuses when it doesn't know instead of answering anyway."

Chapter 13Glossary

Every term used above, in one place.

passage / chunk
One independently searchable piece of text, plus the record of which document and page it came from. Here, 210 tokens.
token
Roughly three-quarters of a word. How models actually count text, and what every size limit is denominated in.
embedding
A list of numbers encoding a text's meaning as a position in space. Here, 384 of them.
bi-encoder
Encodes question and passage separately, so passages can be processed in advance. Fast; never lets the two texts interact.
cross-encoder
Encodes them together for a far better relevance score. Too slow for a whole corpus, hence its use as a second stage.
cosine similarity
The angle between two embeddings. On unit-length vectors it is just their dot product. Range −1 to 1.
BM25
Classic word-overlap scoring, weighting rare terms far above common ones.
IDF
Inverse document frequency — how rare a word is across the corpus, and therefore how much a match on it counts.
hybrid search
Running a meaning-based and a word-based search together, because they fail on different inputs.
RRF
Reciprocal rank fusion. Merges rankings using positions only, never raw scores.
reranking
Re-scoring a shortlist with a slower, more accurate model.
recall vs precision
Recall: is the answer in the pool at all. Precision: is it at the top. The two pipeline stages are judged on different ones.
source recall
Of the documents that could answer, how many came back. The metric most RAG tutorials never track.
context window
The hard limit on how much text a model can be given at once.
parent-document retrieval
Rank on small passages, then return the larger unit around each one.
abstention
Refusing to answer when confidence falls below a calibrated line.
calibrated
The line was chosen from measurements on labelled data, not picked by eye.
golden set
Questions with hand-labelled correct answers, used to score any change.
hallucination
A fluent, confident, false statement. Retrieval-only output cannot produce one, because nothing is composed.
query decomposition
Splitting a question into parts before searching. The unfinished fix for the seven structural failures.
