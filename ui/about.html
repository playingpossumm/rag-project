Why this exists.

A retrieval system built by hand, to find out what the parts actually do.

Most retrieval-augmented generation is assembled from a framework in an afternoon: four imports, a vector store, a prompt. It works, and you learn almost nothing, because every decision that matters was made for you and none of them are visible. This one is built from parts — no framework — and every default in it had to earn its place against a measurement.

The argument What this can offer that a product cannot

This will not beat NotebookLM or Glean on answer quality. They have better models, more compute and teams of people. That is not a weakness to apologise for — it is the reason to build something else. A closed product is a destination: you put a question in and get an answer out, and the middle is not yours to see. The questions below are the ones that middle makes unanswerable.

The discipline is the actual subject. Five times a measurement overturned a plan that sounded obviously right. Prefixing every chunk with its document title was built, measured, and reverted — it cost source recall. An eight-times-larger reranker fixed the same two questions as the small one, ten times slower. Quantising the cross-encoder was 1.5× faster with near-identical rankings and silently made the system refuse questions it could answer. Those are in the engineering log with their numbers, including the ones that made the project worse.

A guide What happens when you ask

Pick a document set, type a question, and the answer comes back with the passage it rests on and the route it took. Nothing is generated by default: what you read is the retrieved text, verbatim. Here is the whole path, in order.

The answers cite their sources by filename. Every document in every set is listed with its title and format, and the whole system is open source — every stage drawn on these pages is a function you can read.

  1. The documents are cut into passages

    Each document is split into overlapping windows of about 210 tokens. Whole documents are too big to compare against a question and too big to read back. Overlap exists so an answer that straddles a boundary is not lost by the cut.

  2. Two retrievers search, at the same moment

    Dense retrieval compares meaning, by turning the question and every passage into vectors. BM25 compares exact words, weighting rare ones heavily. They fail in opposite directions. Dense survives paraphrase and misses model numbers; BM25 finds an identifier instantly and is blind to a question that shares no vocabulary with its answer. Neither is the better one.

  3. The two rankings are fused

    Each retriever contributes 1/(60 + rank) to every passage it returned, and the sums are sorted. Only position is used, never the raw score: cosine similarity and BM25 live on scales that cannot be added without inventing a conversion.

  4. A cross-encoder re-reads the survivors

    The shortlist is scored again by a model that reads the question and the passage together and answers "does this passage answer this question". The first stage compared two summaries made independently. This one actually reads both at once — far more accurate, far too slow to run over a whole corpus, which is why it only sees a shortlist.

  5. At most two passages per document survive

    The diversity cap. Measured as a genuine trade rather than a free win: it costs a little hit rate and buys the answer more of the documents that actually contain it. A question answered by four papers should not return four passages from one.

  6. The gate decides whether to answer at all

    If the best passage scores below this corpus's threshold, the system declines instead of answering. Nearest-neighbour search always returns something. Ask an ornithology corpus about tax law and it will hand back its five least-unrelated passages, at scores that look ordinary. The threshold is calibrated per corpus against questions the documents genuinely cannot answer.

Reading what comes back

The passage is shown verbatim, with only the answering words in bold. The citation names the real document title and the location — a page for a PDF, a slide for a deck, a sheet for a spreadsheet.

The score is a relevance judgement from the cross-encoder, running roughly −11 to +11. It is not a probability and not a percentage. Positive means the passage answers the question directly; strongly negative means the corpus probably does not contain the answer.

“Also found” lists passages from other documents. It is open by default because the top passage is sometimes wrong in a specific way: asked for a bird's fused collarbone, the reranker prefers the pygostyle passage and puts the furcula second.

“The corpus does not contain this” means the gate declined. It is the system working, not failing — and some of the example questions offered on the front page are chosen precisely to trigger it.

Glossary Every term this interface uses
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation. Answering a question by searching a set of documents first and quoting what is found, rather than relying on what a model memorised. retrieval-augmented generation
Corpus
One set of documents, with everything derived from it: its index, its labelled questions, its threshold. Nothing about one corpus transfers to another — the single most reused finding in this project. document set
Chunk
One passage: a window of about 210 tokens cut from a document, with 40 tokens of overlap onto its neighbour. passage
Locator
Where a passage came from, in the unit its format actually has — a page for a PDF, a slide for a deck, a sheet or section elsewhere. Saying “page 3” of a slide deck sends a reader somewhere that does not exist.
Embedding
A passage or question turned into a list of numbers, positioned so that things meaning similar things sit near each other. vector
Dense retrieval
Search by comparing embeddings. Matches meaning, survives paraphrase, and misses rare tokens that carry meaning by identity. vector search · semantic search
BM25
Search by exact term overlap, weighting rare words far above common ones. The exact inverse profile: strong on identifiers, blind to paraphrase. sparse retrieval · lexical search
Fusion
Combining two rankings into one. This system uses reciprocal rank fusion: each retriever contributes 1/(60 + rank), so only position counts and no score conversion is needed. RRF · hybrid search
Candidate pool
The shortlist the first stage hands to the reranker. Its size is a latency dial: reranking is over 90% of the time a query takes and costs scale linearly with it. candidate_k
Cross-encoder
A model that reads a question and a passage together and scores whether one answers the other. Far more accurate than comparing two separately-made embeddings, and far too slow to run over a whole corpus. reranker
Reranking
Re-ordering the shortlist with the cross-encoder. On some corpora a fraction of the first stage's ordering is deliberately kept, because when a question describes a term rather than naming it the cross-encoder has little to work with.
Diversity cap
A limit of two passages per document in the final answer. Trades a little hit rate for covering more of the documents that answer the question.
Context expansion
Attaching the neighbouring passages to each result, so a sentence cut by a chunk boundary is still readable. Cannot change any ranking — it runs after selection.
Abstention threshold
The score below which the system declines to answer. Calibrated per corpus: the same number that costs one corpus a single question costs another ten. the gate · confidence threshold
Golden set
The labelled questions a corpus is scored against — each with the documents and locations that answer it. Every number on the analytics page comes from these.
Adversarial case
A question deliberately included because the documents cannot answer it. Refusing one is a success; answering it is the failure being measured. unanswerable case
any-hit@5
The fraction of questions where at least one correct passage appears in the five returned. Asks "did it find it at all".
MRR
Mean reciprocal rank. Rewards putting the right passage first — 1.0 if it is first, 0.5 if second, and so on. mean reciprocal rank
NDCG
A ranking-quality score over all the relevant passages returned, not just the first, discounting each by how far down it sits. normalised discounted cumulative gain
Source recall
How many of the documents that answer a question were actually returned. The metric hit rate cannot express: returning one of four answering papers scores a perfect hit rate while missing three quarters of the answer.

Built by hand in Python: no retrieval framework, no CDN, no external request from this page. The server binds to localhost on purpose — the documents may be private, and a page about private documents should not call a font host. Fonts are bundled.
